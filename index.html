One word is a complete search. Type into a row exactly as you would in the database’s basic box — OR is optional, and only needed when you want a row to match any of several alternatives. Use + synonym to add one. The line under each row says in plain English what it will match.

What is an ISSN, and why does the bench use it instead of journal names?

An ISSN (International Standard Serial Number) is an eight-digit code that identifies one serial publication, permanently. Journal of Applied Psychology is 0021-9010, and nothing else is. Most journals carry two — a print ISSN and an electronic ISSN for the online edition — and both point at the same journal. The catalogue in section 05 lists both; the bench uses whichever it has.

Why it matters here. SRCTITLE and SO= match any source title that contains your text. So SRCTITLE("Journal of Management") also returns Journal of Management Studies, Journal of Management Inquiry and Journal of Management Education — three different journals, three different editorial boards, three different rating tiers. ISSN(0149-2063) returns exactly one.

The sharpest case is in the catalogue itself: Human Resource Management Journal (UK) is rated 4* and Human Resource Management (USA) is rated 4. They are different journals. No title phrase can separate them; their ISSNs do it instantly.

Your course materials may use SRCTITLE, and that is correct. Elsevier documents it as a field code, and it is the form the Scopus interface generates when you tick journals in the sidebar. ISSN is not a correction — it is a precision upgrade that matters when the journal set is your sample, which is exactly what happens when you sample a rating tier. To produce the form your course uses, set Match journals by to Title phrase and the bench will write SRCTITLE(…) instead.

Operator precedence

    
This is the single biggest difference between the two platforms, and it is the opposite of what most
    people assume. Each database resolves an unbracketed query in a fixed order, and the two orders disagree
    about the most important operator of all.

        A AND (B OR C).

A AND B OR C groups as
        (A AND B) OR C.

      

    


    
The same characters, pasted into the two boxes, describe two different searches. Elsevier has also
    announced that Scopus is moving to the industry-standard order — AND NOT, then
    AND, then OR — rolling out from late 2025 and finalising in early 2026,
    which brings Scopus into line with Web of Science and inverts its own historical behaviour. Section 04
    works through what that does to a real query.


    
The only syntax that is safe across all three regimes is explicit bracketing, which is why the bench
    brackets every expression that mixes operators rather than relying on any platform's defaults.

Phrases, braces and loose matching

    
Scopus distinguishes two kinds of quoting, and the difference is not cosmetic:

    
    
      
  • "job satisfaction" — loose phrase. Punctuation is ignored, and the words are
      required adjacently but the search is still stemmed and lightly normalised. Wildcards inside are ignored.
    • 
      
  • {job satisfaction} — exact string. Matches the literal characters, punctuation
      included. Nothing is stemmed. This is the tool for phrases containing hyphens, slashes or symbols.
    • 
    

    
Web of Science has no brace syntax. Double quotes there mean an exact phrase, and a lone unquoted
    multi-word entry is treated as an implicit phrase in some fields and an implicit AND in others —
    always quote.


    
Truncation

    
Both accept * for zero or more characters and ? for exactly one.
    Web of Science adds $ for zero or one character, which is how you catch
    behavio$r across British and American spelling in a single term. Scopus has no equivalent;
    the bench expands it into an explicit OR pair instead.

    
Short stems are the most common cause of an unmanageable result set. man* retrieves
    man, many, manual, manuscript, manipulate and several thousand other forms. Three characters
    before the asterisk is a floor, not a target.


    
Proximity

    
Proximity operators are the strongest instrument most doctoral searches never use. They capture the
    relationship between two concepts without demanding the exact phrase:

    
    
      
  • Scopus W/n — within n words, either order.
      team W/3 performance catches "team performance" and "performance of the team".
    • 
      
  • Scopus PRE/n — first term precedes the second by at most n words.
    • 
      
  • Web of Science NEAR/n — within n words, either order. There is no
      order-sensitive equivalent, so a PRE/n search cannot be reproduced exactly in WoS.
    • 
      
  • Web of Science SAME — within the same sentence in a topic search, or the same address
      block in an address search. Scopus has no equivalent at all.
    • 
    

    
Asymmetries like these are why a systematic review reports its search string separately for each
    database rather than claiming one query was run everywhere.

What that costs

      
Only the first branch carries the keyword and the year. Every record ever published in the other
      eight journals satisfies the query on its own, back to 1958. A search intended to find productivity
      papers since 2000 returns eight complete back catalogues, and the result set looks entirely plausible
      because the journals are right.

    


    
And from 2026, Scopus behaves this way too

    
In March 2025 Elsevier announced that Scopus is moving to the industry-standard precedence order
    — AND NOT, then AND, then OR — rolling out from late
    2025 and finalising in early 2026. That aligns Scopus with Web of Science and inverts its own
    historical behaviour. Elsevier’s own guidance is to group queries with parentheses so that they
    survive the change.

    
So the same nine-journal query is correct in Scopus before the change, wrong in Web of Science
    throughout, and wrong in Scopus after it — without a single character being edited. If you were
    taught this query, you were taught it correctly for the platform and the period. What changed is the
    platform.

The version that is correct everywhere

    
Brackets are not a matter of taste. They are the only way to write an expression whose meaning does
    not depend on which platform, or which year, is reading it.

    

    

      
      
    


    
A second, quieter defect

    
The source titles in the original are unquoted. Elsevier documents that a loose phrase requires
    straight double quotation marks; without them the words are separate tokens, so
    SRCTITLE(Academy of Management Journal) also matches Academy of Management Review
    and Academy of Management Annals. And even correctly quoted, SRCTITLE and
    SO= match any source title containing the phrase:
    SRCTITLE("Journal of Management") sweeps in Journal of Management Studies,
    Journal of Management Inquiry and Journal of Management Education.

    
The AJG catalogue in section 05 shows how sharp this gets: it lists
    Human Resource Management Journal (UK) at 4* and Human Resource Management (USA) at 4 as
    separate titles. No title-phrase query can separate them.


    
Why the bench writes ISSNs where the sample query writes journal names

    
If you have compared the bench’s output against a query from class, this is the difference you
    will have noticed: where the sample writes SRCTITLE(Academy of Management Journal), the bench
    writes ISSN(0001-4273). Both restrict the search to journals. They do it with different
    precision.


    
What an ISSN is

    
An International Standard Serial Number is an eight-digit code assigned to one serial
    publication and kept for its lifetime. Journal of Applied Psychology is 0021-9010.
    Administrative Science Quarterly is 0001-8392. The number carries no meaning in
    itself — it is an identifier, like a DOI for an article or an ORCID for a person — and the
    final character is a check digit, which is why you will occasionally see one ending in X.

    
Most journals carry two: a print ISSN for the paper edition and an electronic ISSN for the
    online one. Both identify the same journal. Section 05 lists both for every title, and the bench searches
    on whichever it holds. If a search on one returns less than you expect, try the other — indexing is
    not always consistent about which it records.


    
How it limits the search

    
It does the same job as the journal-title clause, in the same position in the query: it is AND-ed to
    your keyword block, so a record must match your topic and appear in one of the listed journals.
    The difference is only in how a journal is named.

    
SRCTITLE("Journal of Management")   →  every source title CONTAINING that phrase
                                        Journal of Management
                                        Journal of Management Studies
                                        Journal of Management Inquiry
                                        Journal of Management Education

ISSN(0149-2063)                     →  Journal of Management

    
For an unambiguous title the two are equivalent and you will see no difference in your results. The gap
    opens on short or heavily reused journal names — which, in management, is most of them.


    

      
The sample query is not wrong

      
SRCTITLE is a documented Scopus field code, and it is the form the Scopus interface
      itself produces when you tick journals in the results sidebar — which is why it appears in
      teaching materials. It is the right tool when you want every journal whose name matches a pattern.

      
ISSN matters when the journal set is your sampling frame, as it is the moment you say
      “the 4* journals”. Then a stray Journal of Management Education in your results is
      not noise to be screened out later — it is a journal that was never in your population, sitting
      inside your sample. That is a defensible-methods problem rather than a syntax problem, and it is the
      reason the bench defaults to ISSN.

      
Follow your course. If your materials use SRCTITLE, set Match journals by to
      Title phrase on the bench and it will generate exactly that form; ISSN where known, else
      title gives you a hybrid for journals the catalogue has no number for.

Why this tool exists

    
Both interfaces make you choose. The row builder is easy to use but cannot express a coded field
    search; the advanced box accepts codes but gives you a blank rectangle and no help. There is no screen in
    either database where you can assemble a search in labelled boxes and see the code it produces.

    
That gap is the whole scope of the bench. It is not a methods course, it does not decide what belongs
    in your search, and it is not a substitute for anything your supervisors or your subject librarian tell
    you. It takes the rows you would have filled in anyway and shows you the syntax underneath, with the
    vendor documentation for every rule it applies. Where this tool and your course disagree, follow your
    course — then tell me, because it means something in the vendor documentation has moved again.


    

      
Before you run anything

      
Field codes and tags are only parsed in the advanced search box — Scopus
      “Advanced document search”, Web of Science “Advanced Search”. Pasted into a
      basic box they are treated as ordinary words and return nonsense. Search through your institution’s
      proxy so that coverage and export limits match your entitlement, and record which interface you used.
